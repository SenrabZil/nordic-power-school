Lesson 03 · Price formation

One unit sets
the price.

The day-ahead auction clears at a single number, and a single generator decides it. Learn to find that unit and you can read any hour.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. We’ll build the price from the ground up — the supply stack, the demand wall, and the one unit where they meet.

01

The auction is a stack

Every generator offers its power at roughly its own cost. Sort all the offers from cheapest to dearest and you get the merit order — a rising staircase of supply. Wind and solar sit at the bottom near zero; hydro and nuclear are low; gas and oil peakers sit at the top.

Demand barely flexes on price, so think of it as a wall standing at the forecast load. Where the wall meets the staircase, that block is the marginal unit — and its offer is the price everyone who cleared gets paid. One number, set at the margin.

03

Why wind floods the curve

Wind bids near zero, so it always enters at the bottom of the stack. More wind doesn’t just add cheap power — it shoves the whole staircase to the right, so the same demand wall now lands on a cheaper step.

Predict: hold demand at that winter-evening level. Now wind picks up hard. What happens to the clearing price?

Push wind far enough and it alone meets demand: the marginal unit becomes wind itself and the price collapses toward zero — the same midday dip you met in Lesson 02, explained from the supply side. (When wind would more than cover demand, prices can even go negative.)

04

From one hour to the whole day

A delivery day is just this calculation run 24 times, as demand and wind change hour to hour. Run the stack across the day and the price curve emerges — no curve drawn by hand, only marginal units.

Predict: demand peaks morning and evening; wind peaks midday. Where is the day’s lowest price?

05

The rule, in one breath

Price is the offer of the marginal unit — where the demand wall meets the supply stack.

Everything that clears is paid that one price. Cheap renewables enter at the bottom and shove the stack right, so more wind means a cheaper marginal unit and a lower price. In the Nordics, hydro’s water value makes its step flexible, so it is often the unit at the margin.
