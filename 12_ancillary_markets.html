Lesson 12 · Ancillary markets

You can’t sell the
same megawatt twice.

Reserves are bought in markets, and the capacity price moves with scarcity. But an asset’s power is finite — so every market it enters is a choice against the others.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. This allocation is the puzzle a battery solves every hour — and the heart of the next lesson.

01

Procurement & the capacity price

The TSO needs a set volume of each reserve. Providers bid their capacity at a €/MW price; the TSO stacks the bids cheapest-first and buys up to the requirement. The last bid taken sets the capacity price everyone clears at — merit order again, but for availability rather than energy.

So the price isn’t fixed. When reserves are scarce — a low-inertia hour needing lots of fast response — the requirement reaches up a thinner stack and the price climbs.

03

The same MW, one job

Ten megawatts of battery. Hold 6 MW in FCR and only 4 remain for the energy spread — the reserved capacity must stay free to respond, so it can’t also be trading. The split that pays most depends entirely on the two prices.

Predict: FCR is paying richly tonight and the energy spread is thin. Where should the 10 MW go?

Drag the prices around and the peak of the revenue curve slides. Cheap FCR and a fat spread? Pour MW into arbitrage. Scarce reserves and a flat day? Hold them in FCR. That moving peak is co-optimisation.

This is how the markets are built and how allocation logic works — not advice on which market to bid into or how much. Qualification, risk and strategy aren’t the school’s to decide.

04

The catches

State of charge is a leash. Symmetric reserves like FCR-N need you ready to go up and down — so you must sit mid-charge, which limits how deep you can arbitrage.
Markets clear in sequence. Energy, then reserve capacity, then closer-to-real-time. You commit before you know everything — allocation under uncertainty.
Opportunity cost is the real price. A MW in FCR costs you the arbitrage it could have earned. Compare margins per MW, not headline prices.
Power and energy both bind. MW limits how much you can offer; MWh limits how long. Reserves and arbitrage compete for both.
05

The rule, in one breath

Reserve prices clear by merit order and scarcity; finite power makes every market a choice against the others.

Capacity is paid where the requirement meets the bid stack, so scarcity lifts the price. An asset then allocates its limited MW to wherever the margin is highest — FCR or the spread — never both with the same megawatt.
