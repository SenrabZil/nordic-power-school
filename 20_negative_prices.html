Lesson 20 · Negative prices

When power
pays to flow.

Sometimes the clearing price falls below zero — generators paying to keep producing. It looks broken. It’s the merit order working exactly as designed, just below the line.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. The fastest-growing hour on the curve, and a direct consequence of subsidy and inflexibility.

01

Why a bid goes below zero

A rational generator bids what it’s willing to be paid to run — usually positive. But two kinds of plant will bid below zero, paying to keep going:

Subsidised renewables earn a payment per megawatt-hour produced — a certificate or contract on top of the market price. As long as that subsidy is larger than the negative price, running still pays, so they’ll bid down to minus their subsidy rather than stop. Must-run plant — a CHP unit obliged to deliver heat, a nuclear block costly and slow to cycle — will pay a little to avoid the larger cost of shutting down and restarting.

Stack those below zero and you have a supply curve that starts in negative territory.

03

The recipe, and the relief valve

Negative prices need a specific coincidence: high renewable output, low demand (night, weekend, mild weather), inflexible supply that won’t switch off, and no way out — the interconnectors already full, so the surplus can’t be exported to a hungrier zone. Slide export relief up and watch the clear climb back above zero: a cable with room is the surplus’s escape, which is why a congested border (Lesson 17) is so often where the deepest negatives appear.

04

What a negative price is telling you

It’s a system shouting that it has too much inflexible supply and nowhere to put it — and paying anyone who can help. A negative price is an invitation: it pays storage to charge, pays flexible demand to switch on, and eventually pays wind to curtail. Every negative hour is the market pricing the value of flexibility the system wishes it had.

More common each year. As subsidised renewables grow and demand stays inflexible, negative hours multiply — reading them is now part of the job.
Spill, in market form. This is Lesson 16’s reservoir hitting the brim, generalised: surplus energy that costs to get rid of.

This explains how and why prices go negative — not advice on trading negative hours or bidding strategy. What to do with a negative price is a decision the school doesn’t make.

05

The rule, in one breath

Prices go negative when inflexible, subsidised supply outruns demand and can’t escape.

Subsidised renewables bid down to minus their subsidy; must-run pays to avoid shutdown; and when low demand clears in that region with the export cables full, the price falls below zero. It isn’t a glitch — it’s the merit order paying for flexibility the system doesn’t have.
