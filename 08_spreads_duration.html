A flat day pays nothing for moving energy in time. A day with a deep dip and a tall peak pays plenty. Learn to see the spread — and how many hours of it you can reach.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. This is the curve every battery and arbitrage book lives on. Read it well and the next two lessons are easy.

01

Spread, not level

The value in time-shifting energy is the spread — the distance between the day’s cheap hours and its dear hours. A day that sits flat at €90 is expensive but worthless to shift; a day that dips to €20 and peaks at €85 is cheaper on average yet full of opportunity.

What opens the spread? The residual-load swing from Lesson 07: wind floods the midday trough, evening demand lifts the peak. Wide residual swing, wide spread.

03

The capture window

A store buys the cheapest hours and sells the dearest. On the duration curve that’s the right end (charge) against the left end (discharge) — and the vertical gap between them is what one cycle earns per MWh, before any losses.

Predict: drag storage to 4 hours. What gross spread does a single cycle reach — the gap between the four dearest and four cheapest hours?

Notice the diminishing returns: widen storage to 8 hours and the per-MWh spread falls — you’re reaching into less extreme prices. The first hours of storage are the most valuable.

This shows where value exists in a day’s shape. It is not a dispatch schedule or trade advice — real stores face round-trip losses, cycle limits, and dispatch rules. We add those next.

04

Reading spreads honestly

Spread ≠ level. A cheap volatile day beats an expensive flat one for anything that shifts energy.
The duration curve hides the clock. It tells you how many hours, not which — keep the by-hour view beside it to know when.
First hours pay most. Storage value per MWh falls as you add hours; the extreme peak and trough are reached first.
Shape comes from fundamentals. No residual swing, no spread. Read the wind and demand to anticipate the shape.
05

The rule, in one breath

Value lives in the spread, and the duration curve measures it.

Sort the day to read how many hours sit above any level; the gap between the dearest and cheapest hours is what a cycle can reach, with the first hours worth the most. Shape, not level — and shape is the residual swing you already know how to read.
