Lesson 16 · Hydro

The water has
a price.

Most Nordic power is stored as water behind a dam. The price a producer puts on releasing it — the water value — is the quiet hand behind half the curve.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. Nothing explains Nordic prices more than reservoirs, snowmelt, and the value of holding water.

01

A reservoir is a battery

A full Nordic reservoir holds energy measured in terawatt-hours — a battery the size of a country, charged by rain and snowmelt. Unlike a thermal plant, hydro’s “fuel” is free but finite and timed: every megawatt-hour released now is one that can’t be sold into a scarcer hour later.

So the producer doesn’t bid a fuel cost. It bids an opportunity cost.

02

The water value

The water value is what a unit of stored water is worth if held for the future instead of sold now. The rule is simple: release if today’s price is above the water value, hold if below. So hydro bids in at its water value — and across a wide band of hours, that bid is the marginal price. Hydro is the swing producer that smooths the curve.

And the water value isn’t fixed. It rises when water is scarce and falls when it’s abundant — which is a story told by the reservoir and the season.

03

Dry years and wet years

Toggle the year type. A dry year — thin inflow, reservoirs that never refill — lifts the water value across every month: scarcity is priced in all year, and a dry autumn is a high-price winter waiting to happen. A wet year does the opposite, and at the extreme the reservoir hits the brim: with more water arriving than can be stored, the producer faces spill — water wasted over the top — so the water value collapses toward zero and prices sink with it.

This is the engine under Lesson 09’s dry-year button: the forward curve lifts in a dry year because the whole water-value path lifts. Read the reservoir, and you’re reading months of price at once.

04

The soft floor and ceiling

Because hydro will not sell below its water value (it holds) and will not hold above it (it releases), the water value acts as a soft floor and ceiling on price — the reason Nordic prices are often calmer than a pure thermal system. When you see a price that won’t fall further on a windy night, you’re often watching hydro defend its water value. When it collapses anyway, suspect spill.

This explains how the water value shapes price — it is not advice on when to release, hold, or trade hydro. Reservoir strategy is a producer’s decision the school doesn’t make.

05

The rule, in one breath

Hydro bids its water value — the price of holding water for later — and that bid sets much of the curve.

The water value rises as reservoirs fall (scarce) and sinks as they fill (abundant, even to spill). Low reservoirs and dry inflow mean a high water value and firm prices; full reservoirs mean a low one. Read the reservoir and the season, and you read the floor under the market.
