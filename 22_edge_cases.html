Lesson 22 · Edge cases

When the
pattern breaks.

The school teaches one shape: an overnight low, a midday wind trough, an evening peak, a clean spread. Real days don’t always read that way. Knowing which force bent the curve — and which cue to trust — is the difference between reading a market and reciting a pattern.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. Twelve days the textbook day doesn’t cover. Call each one before it reveals.

Builds on · 03 Price formation · 07 Reading curves

01

The shape you’ve been taught

Across the curriculum the chain has been the same: demand and wind give residual load; residual load walks the merit order; the clear follows. On the canonical day that makes a recognisable curve — quiet overnight, a midday dip as wind floods cheap supply, a climb to the evening demand peak. Each sketch below shows that chain directly: the day’s price on top, and beneath it the two cues — wind, and residual load (demand minus wind).

An edge case is a day where one link in that chain is overridden by a specific force: the wind doesn’t blow, a cable goes dark, hydro floods the stack, the sun does the dipping, or the clock itself changes length. Each one below is masked until you make the call — predict first, then reveal.

Each sketch is a stylised shape, labelled with the zone and the day it’s drawn after. Two of them you can open as live drills.

This lesson is reading practice. Each day is here to be recognised and understood, never traded. Nothing here is a bid, a position, or a signal to act — an edge case is a thing to read, not a thing to chase.

02

The negative floor

SE2Sun 11 May 2025representative

Shape holds · numbers don’t

A windy, low-demand day — a mild spring weekend — pushes residual load so low the clear falls through zero. Subsidised wind and must-run plant bid below the line rather than stop (Lesson 20), so the stack starts in negative territory and the midday wind trough lands beneath it. The shape is the textbook one; only the numbers are unusual.

Predict first: wind flooding in, demand at its floor, the cables already full. Where does the midday clear?

Make the call to reveal the day.
SE2 · 11 May 2025. ━ price · ━ wind · ┄ residual — wind floods midday, residual drops below zero, price follows.

Elizabeth: the read barely changes — wind carves the midday, demand lifts the evening. What changes is that the trough is a price you’re paid to sit at. Recognise the curve doing the ordinary thing in an extraordinary place; nothing has broken.

Seen recently: SE2 logged the most negative-price hours of any European zone in 2025 — on the order of a month of hours — in spring snowmelt, windy shoulder seasons and low-demand weekends and nights.

03

The Dunkelflaute

FIWed 8 Jan 2025representative

Pattern breaks

Dunkelflaute is German — Dunkel, dark; Flaute, a lull or calm — a “dark wind-lull”. It names a winter stretch, sometimes lasting several days, when a stalled high-pressure system brings little wind and little sun at once: short, overcast days and still, cold air. Wind and solar fail together at exactly the moment heating demand is highest. For a renewable-heavy grid it is the hardest day of the year to serve.

For a reader, it’s the day the template misleads. The cue you’re taught to look for — wind carving a midday dip — is simply absent, so nothing suppresses the middle of the day. Price stays high throughout; the real low is overnight, and the high is the breakfast heating ramp.

Predict first: on a cold, windless day, where does the price top out?

Make the call to reveal the day.
FI · 8 Jan 2025. ━ price · ━ wind · ┄ residual — wind flat near zero, so no midday dip; price tracks the residual humps.

Elizabeth: this is the day the template misleads. Read it the canonical way and you’ll hunt a midday dip the weather never made. Read the cue — wind near zero — and the shape explains itself.

Seen recently: repeated low-wind cold spells across Europe in early 2025. Finland and the Baltics ran cold with little wind in January 2025, prices well above the prior year and climbing further in February.

04

The decoupled zone

EE vs FIWed 12 Feb 2025observed

Pattern breaks

Zones normally couple: the interconnectors flow until prices converge, and a zone’s curve looks like its neighbours’. When a cable trips, that link is severed. The zone falls back on its own stack — local thermal, costlier imports — and can sit far above a cheap neighbour for days or months. The single-zone curve stops telling you anything about the region.

Predict first: Estonia’s cable to Finland is down; Finland is calm and cheap. Where does Estonia’s price sit?

Make the call to reveal the day.
EE vs FI · 12 Feb 2025. ━ Estonia (islanded)   ━ Finland — the band is the gap the cable would erase.

Elizabeth: the tell is the gap, not the shape. When one zone runs persistently above an otherwise-similar neighbour, ask what stopped the flow between them before you ask anything about merit order — the cable, not the stack, is setting the price.

Seen recently: the Estlink 2 cable between Finland and Estonia was severed on 25 December 2024 and stayed out until around August 2025. Baltic prices rose more than 40%; on 12 February 2025 Estonia’s average exchange price ran roughly four and a half times Finland’s.

05

The hydro-flooded flat

NO4Mon 14 Jul 2025representative

Pattern breaks

A wet year with full reservoirs floods cheap, flexible hydro across every hour. Hydro’s water value (Lesson 16) sits low and even, capping the clear at much the same level morning, noon and night. Volatility collapses — there’s a trough and peak only in the most technical sense, and the spread is too thin to cover a round-trip’s losses.

Predict first: reservoirs are brimming after a wet spring. What does the day’s spread look like?

Make the call to reveal the day.
NO4 · 14 Jul 2025. ━ price · ━ wind · ┄ residual — residual still swings, but abundant hydro pins price flat.

Elizabeth: the capstone’s arbitrage stage will tell you, correctly, that this day doesn’t pay to cycle. That isn’t a failure to find the trade — it’s the right read. Some days the honest answer is that there’s nothing to do.

Seen recently: through 2025, very low prices in the northern Nordics on a strong hydrological balance — abundant reservoirs pressing the system price sharply lower year on year.

06

The solar-led dip

DK1Sun 8 Jun 2025representative

Right shape · wrong cause

The subtle one. In Denmark, southern Sweden and the Baltics, growing solar now carves a midday trough of its own, with a steep ramp as the sun drops into the evening peak. The curve looks almost exactly like the canonical wind day, so a student reading “wind” gets the right answer for the wrong reason. The truth is in the cue panel: low wind, a midday solar bulge.

Predict first: the price dips hard at midday and climbs to an evening peak — same shape as a wind day. Which cue actually carved the dip?

Make the call to reveal the day.
DK1 · 8 Jun 2025. ━ price · ━ wind · ━ solar · ┄ residual — wind is flat, but the solar bulge pulls residual (demand − wind − solar) down at midday: the sun carved it.

Elizabeth: this is why we read the cues, not the price. A day that looks right can still be misread — wind is flat all day, yet the price dips; only the solar line explains it. Think “wind” here and you’ll be wrong tomorrow, when the sun is out and the wind is the same.

Seen recently: negative Nordic prices in Q2 2025 occurred specifically during high-solar hours, and across Europe solar became the single largest source of electricity for the first time in June 2025.

07

The day that isn’t 24 hours

All zonesSun 26 Oct 2025observed

Structure breaks

The last edge isn’t about price — it’s the clock. Two structural facts break any tool that assumes a flat 24 hourly slots. At the daylight-saving transitions a day has 23 or 25 hours: on 26 October 2025 the clocks went back and the 02:00–03:00 hour repeated. And since 1 October 2025 the Nordic day-ahead clears in 15-minute market time units — 96 periods a day, not 24.

Predict first: it’s the autumn clock change. How many hours does this delivery day have?

Make the call to reveal the day.
26 Oct 2025. the autumn change carries an extra hour; the 15-minute MTU carries four periods where the lessons draw one.

Elizabeth: never index a day by hour-of-day, and never assume it’s 24 long. Carry each period’s own length and timestamp, store and compute in UTC, convert to market time only at the edges — exactly as Lesson 2 insisted. The clock is a cue too.

When a day repeats an hour or runs on quarter-hours, a “trough” or “peak” found by counting slots can land on the wrong moment. Read the timestamps, not the position in the list.

08

The scarcity spike

FIFri 5 Jan 2024observed

Shape holds · numbers don’t

The mirror image of the negative floor. A deep freeze pushes demand toward record levels while a windless high-pressure system leaves wind near zero. Residual load runs so high that the clear climbs deep into the costliest peaking plant, and price vaults toward the cap. The shape is still a textbook evening peak — only the altitude is extraordinary.

Predict first: deep freeze, demand near record, almost no wind. Where does the evening peak clear?

Make the call to reveal the day.
FI · 5 Jan 2024. ━ price · ━ wind · ┄ residual — wind flat near zero, residual near record, price near the cap.

Elizabeth: read it exactly like the canonical day — overnight low, evening peak — then respect the scale. The same grammar can spell a number an order of magnitude past anything the curriculum’s sample days show.

Seen recently: on 5 January 2024 a prolonged Arctic freeze with little wind pushed Finland’s day-ahead average toward €890/MWh, with the evening peak near €1,900/MWh. Fingrid raised its readiness and urged consumers to shift load; imports from Sweden and Estonia were maxed out.

09

The unit trip

FISun 17 Nov 2024observed

Structure breaks mid-day

A single large generator can leave the stack in seconds. When it does, that block of cheap capacity vanishes and the zone climbs to costlier plant within the hour — an instant upward step that no weather cue foretold. The day reads perfectly normal right up to the moment it doesn’t.

Predict first: a 1,600 MW reactor trips offline at 15:25. What happens to price?

Make the call to reveal the day.
FI · 17 Nov 2024. ━ price · ━ wind · ┄ residual — the trip steps residual up; price follows within the hour.

Elizabeth: this is the cue that isn’t weather at all. Watch availability, not just demand and wind — a healthy-looking curve can break the instant a big unit drops.

Seen recently: on 17 November 2024 Olkiluoto 3 — Europe’s largest reactor at 1,600 MW — tripped on a turbine fault, briefly dragging Nordic system frequency to about 49.59 Hz and lifting Finnish and Nordic short-term prices while the outage ran for several days.

10

The forecast bust

Any zonerepresentativerepresentative

Right read · wrong day

The day-ahead clears on forecasts. If it priced in an afternoon of wind that never arrived, the realised system is short: intraday prices climb to source the missing energy, and imbalance prices punish the gap after the fact. The day-ahead curve wasn’t misread — it was a fair read of a day that didn’t happen.

Predict first: day-ahead priced in a windy afternoon. The wind never came. Where do intraday and imbalance prices go?

Make the call to reveal the day.
Representative. ━ realised / intraday · ━ day-ahead — the band is the shortfall the missing wind created.

Elizabeth: the day-ahead curve is a forecast wearing a price. When the weather diverges, the truth shows up in the intraday and imbalance markets (Lessons 18–19) — not in the number you read the night before.

Seen recently: Fingrid has repeatedly flagged wind landing far from forecast — sometimes well above a low-wind scenario, sometimes cut sharply by blade icing. Every miss is settled in the intraday and imbalance markets, not the day-ahead.

11

The flow reversal

FI ↔ EESat 2 Mar 2024observed

The link flips

An interconnector has a direction, and it isn’t fixed. A cable that carries power one way all winter can reverse when the exporting zone loses generation: the importer becomes the exporter, or the reverse. The coupling itself changes sign — and a single-zone read misses it entirely.

Predict first: Finland exported to Estonia all winter. Its biggest reactor goes down for maintenance. The interconnector now…

Make the call to reveal the day.
FI ↔ EE · 2 Mar 2024. net flow on the Finland–Estonia link — above the line is export, below is import.

Elizabeth: never read a border as a fixed arrow. When a zone loses supply, the cable that fed its neighbour can turn around and feed it instead — the direction is a variable, not a label.

Seen recently: when Olkiluoto 3 went offline for maintenance on 2 March 2024, Finland flipped from exporting a few hundred MW to Estonia to importing a similar amount — the flow reversed with the loss of supply.

12

The fuel-shock level shift

Europe / FIDec 2022observed

Shape holds · level doesn’t

Gas and carbon set the marginal cost of the thermal plant at the top of the stack. When they jump, every hour that the marginal unit is thermal lifts with them — so the whole curve rises, overnight low and evening peak alike, without changing shape. You’re reading the same day at a different altitude.

Predict first: gas and carbon prices roughly triple. The daily price curve…

Make the call to reveal the day.
Europe / FI · Dec 2022. ━ fuel-shock level · ━ normal level — same shape, lifted bodily.

Elizabeth: separate the shape from the level. Weather and demand draw the curve’s shape; fuel and carbon set its height. A level shift is not a new pattern — it’s the same pattern, re-priced.

Seen recently: through the 2022 gas crisis the curve kept its familiar shape but sat far higher — Finland’s December 2022 average was about €246/MWh against roughly €60/MWh the following spring, and the European benchmark briefly topped €400/MWh on gas and carbon costs.

13

The holiday profile

FIWed 1 May 2024observed

The demand shape breaks

The morning ramp you lean on is people: commuters and industry waking up together. On a public holiday that ramp simply doesn’t happen. Demand stays low and flat all day, so the price loses its usual landmarks — the “morning peak” you’d reach for isn’t there to find.

Predict first: it’s a public holiday. Where’s the morning demand ramp?

Make the call to reveal the day.
FI · 1 May 2024. ━ price · ━ wind · ┄ residual — flat holiday demand, no morning ramp, shapeless price.

Elizabeth: the calendar is a cue too. Before you reach for “morning peak”, ask whether anyone’s actually going to work — on a holiday the demand shape you assume just isn’t there.

Seen recently: on 1 May 2024 — Vappu, a Finnish public holiday — demand stayed flat and subdued. The very next day it jumped roughly 800 MW as normal activity resumed and prices climbed back to a workday shape.

14

Build the day

Reading is half of it; the other half is knowing which force does what. This is a stylised model — a teaching toy, not a market — that turns the chain the right way round: you set the forces, and the price follows. Add wind and watch the midday sink. Switch on solar and watch a second dip appear with no wind at all. Flood the system with hydro and watch the whole curve go flat. Trip a unit offline and watch the stack climb.

━ price · ━ wind · ━ solar · ┄ residual — residual = demand − wind − solar (a tripped unit adds to it).

A toy to build intuition, not a forecast or a dispatch tool. The numbers are illustrative; nothing here is a bid, a position, or a signal to act.

15

Read the force, not the template

The canonical day is a starting grammar, not a law. Every edge here is the same lesson from a different angle: the cues come first. When the shape surprises you, don’t bend the day to fit the pattern — find the force that bent it.

No wind and the midday dip vanishes. A dead cable and the zone leaves the region behind. A flood of hydro and the whole curve flattens. Solar instead of wind and the right shape hides the wrong cause. A clock change and the day isn’t even 24 hours long. A deep freeze vaults price toward the cap; a tripped unit climbs the stack in minutes; a fuel shock lifts the whole curve; a holiday erases the morning ramp; a busted forecast is settled intraday; and a lost reactor can reverse a cable’s flow.

Read which of those is in charge today, and the day — however strange — explains itself.
