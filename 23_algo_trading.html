Lesson 23 · Algorithmic trading

The machines
in the market.

Most of the order flow in the fast Nordic markets is now placed and managed by software, not by hand. This lesson is about what that machinery is, where it lives, and what it does to the prices you’ve learned to read.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. How it works, and how it bends the market — never how to build one.

01

Who’s actually trading now?

A decade ago a power trader watched screens and clicked. Today, in the markets closest to delivery, most orders are submitted, modified and cancelled by software running unattended — far faster, and far more often, than any person could manage.

The reason is the rest of this course. More wind and solar means more forecast revisions, which means more reasons to re-trade after the day-ahead auction has closed. Finer products — the move to 15-minute periods — multiply the number of contracts in play. Pan-European coupling links dozens of zones at once. The volume and speed of decisions outran human hands, so the execution was handed to machines. The judgement — the strategy, the limits, the switch — still belongs to people.

This lesson explains how that machinery works and what it does to the market. It is not a guide to building or running a trading algorithm, and nothing in it is a strategy, a signal, or advice to trade. As always in this school, we’re here to read the market, not to act in it.

02

Anatomy of an algorithm

Strip away the mystique and a trading algorithm is three parts joined in a loop: a feed of data coming in, a logic that decides what to do with it, and an order action going out — after which it watches its own fills and the changing market and goes round again.

The same loop runs from the simplest rule to the most complex model — only the logic box changes.

The simplest logic is a rule a human could state in a sentence: if this condition holds, place that order. The most complex re-solves an entire portfolio optimisation every few seconds. Either way the difference from manual trading isn’t intelligence — it’s that the loop runs on its own, inside limits a person set in advance.

Elizabeth: the word “algo” sounds exotic, but it’s just a decision written down precisely enough that a computer can follow it without you standing there. The judgement still came from a human — once.

03

Where the algorithms live

Nordic power trades in three arenas, and automation isn’t spread evenly across them.

Predict first: of day-ahead, intraday and balancing, which arena runs the most on automation?

Make the call to reveal the three arenas.
The closer to delivery, the more the machines: day-ahead is one careful auction; intraday and balancing run on automation.

Day-ahead is a single blind auction each day, clearing around midday for the next day — you can prepare a bid curve carefully, at human pace. Intraday (the pan-European SIDC) is a continuous order book open through the day right up to near delivery, complemented by three daily auctions; this is the arena that rewards speed and runs heavily on software. Balancing is real time, on the European platforms the TSOs operate — the most automated and the closest to delivery of all.

For scale: continuous intraday across Europe ran on the order of seventy-five million trades in a single quarter of 2025, and the three intraday auctions have operated since June 2024 on 15-minute products.

04

The families of algorithm

“Algo” isn’t one thing. A handful of broad families do most of the work, and a single desk’s system often blends several.

Execution algos take a position a human decided on and slice it into smaller orders over time, so a large trade doesn’t move the price against itself.
Market-making algos quote both a buy and a sell price continuously, earning the spread between them and providing the liquidity that others trade against.
Arbitrage algos spot the same megawatt priced differently in two places or two timeframes — day-ahead versus intraday, one zone versus its neighbour — and trade the gap until it closes.
Forecast-driven algos ingest each new weather, load or outage update and re-price positions the instant the fundamentals shift — the cues from across this course, read by machine.
Asset-optimisation algos steer a physical asset — a battery above all, also hydro or flexible demand — deciding when to charge, hold or deliver across all three arenas, within the market rules.

Elizabeth: notice that none of these invents information. They react to the same forecasts, flows and outages you’ve been learning to read — they just react faster, and never blink.

05

Why seconds matter

Power isn’t equities, where edges are measured in microseconds. But in the continuous intraday book, a few seconds is a real advantage — and that, more than anything, is why the work is automated.

Predict first: a wind forecast is revised downward at 13:02. When do intraday prices first move?

Within minutes — usually seconds. The moment a forecast is published, every participant’s view of residual load shifts at the same time. In the continuous book the fastest systems re-quote before slower hands have finished reading the update, so the price has already moved by the time a person looks. The information is public; the only question is who has re-priced first.
Make the call to reveal the answer.

Elizabeth: speed here isn’t about being clever — it’s about not being last. You and the machine read the same public number; the machine has simply finished reading it sooner.

06

Effect: liquidity and efficiency

Two effects are mostly to the good. Liquidity: market-making algos quote almost continuously, so there is nearly always a price to trade against, and spreads are tighter than a human-only book would hold. Efficiency: new information — a forecast revision, an outage, a change in cross-border flow — reaches the price within seconds, and arbitrage algos pull prices in different markets and zones toward one another until the gaps close.

Two venues, one product: arbitrage algos trade the gap until the prices meet.

The net is a market that absorbs news quickly and, on a normal day, prices it consistently across venues. The same speed that does this is also what makes the rare day sharper — which is the next section.

07

Effect: volatility, the double edge

The honest answer to “do the machines calm the market or stir it up?” is the one people least like.

Predict first: do algorithms make power prices more volatile, or less?

Both — and which one shows up depends on the day. On an ordinary day, deep automated market-making and fast arbitrage damp small dislocations: a brief spike is sold into and fades. But algorithms reading the same public signal tend to react the same way at the same instant. In a thin book — overnight, or just before gate closure — those correlated reactions can gap the price hard. Automation calms the routine and can sharpen the exceptional.
Make the call to reveal the answer.

This is why “the algos will smooth it” is never a safe assumption. The same speed that settles a normal day can accelerate a bad one — and a mis-coded or runaway algorithm can do real damage before anyone reacts. Hence the rules in the next section.

08

Batteries, flexibility and the 15-minute world

Since the market moved to 15-minute products, a delivery day holds ninety-six periods, each tradable across several venues — well past what a person can manage by hand. So the flexible assets that thrive on that granularity (batteries above all, plus hydro and demand response) are increasingly steered by optimisation algos that value-stack across day-ahead, intraday and balancing.

That matters for everything else in this course. Those automated, flexible assets are now often the marginal price-setter in the intraday and balancing arenas — which means automated flexibility is starting to shape the very curves the rest of the school teaches you to read.

09

The rules of the game

Automated trading is regulated, not a free-for-all. Under REMIT II — the revised EU regulation on wholesale-energy market integrity, in force since 7 May 2024 — algorithmic trading is defined as software that decides the individual parameters of an order (whether to place it, when, at what price and quantity, and how to manage it afterwards) with little or no human intervention, high-frequency trading included.

With that come duties. A firm must notify its regulator — the EU agency ACER and its national authority — that it trades this way; it must design its algorithms to be resilient and not to disrupt the market; and it must keep records of how they behave. Market manipulation — layering or spoofing the order book, withholding capacity to move a price — is prohibited whether a human or a machine does it. A machine does not dilute responsibility; the person who built it and switched it on still owns the conduct.

Elizabeth: the regulator’s question is never “was it the algo?” It’s “who built it, who turned it on, and could they stop it?” Resilience and a working kill switch aren’t paperwork — they’re the price of automating.

10

A machine trades faster; the market still clears the same way

Algorithms don’t repeal the physics or the merit order. They read the same cues you’ve been learning — forecasts, outages, flows, the clock — only faster, more often, and without sleeping.

Most of the time they make the fast markets more liquid and more efficient. When every model reads one signal the same way, they can make a thin moment sharper. Understand what they’re reacting to and an automated market is still legible.

The machine is fast. The fundamentals are unchanged.
