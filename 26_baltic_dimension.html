corner of the map.

This is a Nordic and Baltic school, and in 2025 the Baltic grid changed more than any other in Europe — cutting the last cord to the Soviet-era system and joining the continent. Here’s what that means for the price.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. The synchronisation, the cables, and why this corner decouples so readily.

Builds on · 04 Zonal pricing

01

Where the Baltics sit

Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania are three small, increasingly renewable systems — zones EE, LV and LT in the wider Nord Pool area. For decades they were an “energy island”: electrically tied to the post-Soviet BRELL ring (Belarus, Russia, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania), a synchronous grid whose frequency was controlled from Moscow. They traded in European markets, but the physical heartbeat of their grid was set elsewhere.

That mattered more than it sounds. The country that controls a synchronous area’s frequency controls its balancing — the second-by-second matching of supply and demand. The Baltics priced like Europe but balanced like the old bloc.

02

The 2025 switch

After fifteen years of planning and roughly €1.6 billion of investment, that ended. On 8 February 2025 the three states disconnected from BRELL, severing the last electrical link to Russia and Belarus. They ran for a day as an isolated “island,” proving they could hold their own frequency, and on 9 February 2025 synchronised with the Continental European grid — the largest synchronous system in the world. It went smoothly: no blackouts, consumers unaffected.

8–9 February 2025. Out of BRELL, a day alone, into Continental Europe — the last Soviet-era grid tie in the EU, cut.
03

Why the synchronous area matters

A synchronous area shares one frequency, and its members share the job of holding it. By joining the continent, the Baltics now draw balancing and reserves from European partners instead of Moscow, and a new Baltic frequency-reserve market opened alongside the switch to make that flexibility worth supplying. The ancillary machinery of Lessons 5 and 11 — reserves, frequency, the 50 Hz heartbeat — now runs on European rules and European partners.

Elizabeth: day-ahead coupling already made the Baltics trade like Europe. Synchronisation made them balance like Europe. The market joined years ago; in 2025 the physics finally caught up.

04

The wires that link them to the Nordics

Baltic prices couple to the Nordics through a short list of cables, and the whole region’s price behaviour hangs on them: Estlink 1 and 2 across the Gulf of Finland (Finland–Estonia), NordBalt between Sweden’s SE4 and Lithuania, and LitPol Link to Poland. With so few links and such small systems, the loss of any one cable moves prices hard.

Thin by design. A handful of cables carry the Baltics’ entire coupling to their neighbours.

Predict first: Estlink 2, the larger Finland–Estonia cable, is severed. Finland is calm and cheap. What happens to Estonian prices?

They jump above Finland. Cut the cheap import route and Estonia falls back on its own costlier generation — the zone decouples and prices climb. This is exactly what happened: Estlink 2 was severed on 25 December 2024 and stayed out for months, and on 12 February 2025 Estonia’s average price ran roughly four and a half times Finland’s. The cable, not the stack, set the gap (Lesson 22).

05

Security as a market force

In the Baltic Sea, the cables themselves have become a price driver. Estlink 2 was severed on Christmas Day 2024 in a suspected sabotage; a suspected attempt on NordBalt followed in January 2025; the Poland link was put under guard ahead of synchronisation. Around the switch, the commercial capacity of the Nordic interconnectors was deliberately held back as reserve — NordBalt only returned to full commercial trading later in February — so even undamaged cables carried less.

The practical reading: in this region an interconnector outage isn’t a rare edge case, it’s a recurring feature. Geopolitics shows up directly in the cross-border spread.

06

Reading the Baltics

Put it together and the Baltic zones behave like a more volatile, more decoupling-prone version of the Nordic core: small systems, a rising renewable share, and thin links that are occasionally cut. The same cues you’ve learned still apply — demand, wind, residual load, the marginal plant — but the interconnector status sits on top of all of them as the first thing to check.

Predict first: compared with a large, well-connected zone like SE3, is an Estonian price likely to be more or less volatile?

More volatile. A small system has less internal generation to cushion a shock, and thin interconnection means a single cable outage can island it from cheap neighbours. The same wind lull or plant trip that nudges a big, well-linked zone can swing a small, thinly-connected one hard. Read the cable before you read the zone.

07

A young grid, read with old tools

The Baltics now price and balance as part of Europe — the last Soviet-era cord cut in February 2025. But small systems and thin, exposed cables make this the most decoupling-prone corner of the map.

Everything the school taught still holds here — demand and wind draw the shape, the marginal plant sets the level. There’s just one extra question to ask first, every time: are the cables whole?

Read the interconnector, then read the zone.
