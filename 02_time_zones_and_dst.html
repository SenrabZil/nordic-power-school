Lesson 02 · Time

Time is not what
it looks like.

Most of the ugliest bugs in power data are time bugs. Before you read a single price, get fluent in three clocks — and in the days that aren’t 24 hours long.

with Elizabeth Cecilia, your mentor. When I ask you to predict, actually predict — before you reveal. Guessing then checking is the whole point.

01

One instant, three faces

There is exactly one truth: UTC. We store and compute in it, and convert to a local clock only at the edge. Two local clocks matter here: CET, the clock the coupled day-ahead market runs on, and EET — Finland’s, one hour ahead of CET.

Drag the moment. Switch winter to summer. Watch the same instant wear three different faces — and watch the zone names flip to CEST / EEST when daylight saving lands.

None is “more correct” — but only UTC is unambiguous. A timestamp with no zone attached is a bug until proven otherwise.

02

The delivery day is CET — even in Finland

Finland lives on EET, but its day-ahead delivery day is set by the coupled market, in CET. So the Finnish day-ahead “day” does not start at Finnish midnight. Line up the same hours on all three clocks and the gap jumps out:

Day-ahead delivery day 2026-01-10 begins at 23:00Z = 01:00 in Helsinki — an hour after Finnish midnight (22:00Z).

Slice an FI series by “Finnish calendar day” and then compare it to a CET delivery day, and you are off by one hour on every record. Choose your clock on purpose.

03

Know your source’s clock

Two Fingrid systems, two clocks. Don’t conflate them.

Fingrid Open Data API

The feed this school reads. Timestamps are UTC, in and out (...Z). No conversion on ingest.

Fingrid Datahub

The retail metering exchange — a different system. Reports in EET. Not what we read here.

Before you parse any feed, find the sentence in its docs that names the timezone. If you can’t, assume nothing and test against a known instant.

04

The days that aren’t 24 hours

Twice a year the clock jumps. We never assume 24 periods and never index by hour-of-day — we derive the count from the actual span.

Predict first: on the last Sunday of October, when CET clocks go back, how long is that delivery day?

24 hours · 96 quarter-hours

Any code that hard-codes 24 (or 96) is wrong two days a year — and those are exactly the days with the wildest prices, so the bug hides where it hurts most.

05

The gate: what was knowable when

Day-ahead is an auction. Bids close at the gate (~12:00 CET on D−1), the market couples, and prices publish after. So at the moment you’d form a view, tomorrow’s price does not exist yet — only what published before the gate. Below, the wind forecast is known. The cleared price is behind the curtain.

Predict: wind ramps hard into midday and eases by evening. Where does the day-ahead price bottom out?

The forecast was in your hands; the price was not, until you revealed it. That gap is the whole game — and it’s why a price’s publication time must sit after the gate.

06

The rule, in one breath

Store and compute in UTC.

Convert to a local clock only at the edge, and choose that clock on purpose — CET for the day-ahead delivery day, EET for Finnish local framing. Carry the period resolution and the publication time on every record. Never count hours by hand, and never assume a day is 24 of them.
