Nordic Intraday: the Auction and the Book

Day-ahead gives you one price for an hour. Intraday gives you two entirely different ways of finding a price — and one of them refuses to give you a single number at all. This lesson is about why.

LEVEL · INTERMEDIATE PREREQUISITE · DAY-AHEAD MENTOR · ELIZABETH CECILIA ~25 MIN
Education only

This is a teaching environment. Nothing here is trading advice, a recommendation, or a signal to act on. We read what moves a price; we never tell you what to do with it. Every number below is synthetic and deterministic — built to illustrate market structure, not to reflect any live market.

Where we are

Why intraday exists at all

Yesterday afternoon the day-ahead auction cleared. For every delivery hour tomorrow, every bidding zone got a single price, and everyone planned around it. Clean.

Then reality starts moving. The wind forecast for tomorrow evening softens. A nuclear unit in SE3 trips. Your customers' demand comes in higher than your model said. The day-ahead position you locked in no longer matches the power you'll actually need or produce — and delivery is getting closer by the hour.

Intraday is the adjustment market. It runs after day-ahead has cleared and lets participants trade their way back into balance as information arrives, right up close to real time. Where day-ahead is one big synchronized auction, intraday has to cope with a continuous trickle of new information — and that shapes everything about how it works.

BEAT 01 — READ THE DRIVER

A forecast moves. Which way does the price go?

It's 13:00. Day-ahead for tomorrow has long since cleared. A fresh weather update drops the wind forecast for delivery hour 18 by 2 GW across the region.

Predict — before you read on

Which way do intraday prices for hour 18 move, and through whose hand?

Don't reach for a number. Just reason it through: who is now in the wrong position, and what must they do about it?

Reveal

Less wind means less cheap generation for hour 18 than the market planned for. Anyone who sold power day-ahead expecting that wind is now short — they have a delivery obligation and less supply behind it. To stay balanced they must buy power back in intraday for hour 18.

A wave of buyers chasing a now-scarcer hour lifts the price. So: intraday firms upward, driven by short positions covering. Notice what we did — we read the driver (a supply shock) and the mechanism (who must trade and why), not a chart. That's the habit this whole school is built on.

The two mechanisms

Intraday isn't one market — it's two

Under the Single Intraday Coupling (SIDC) framework, the Nordic intraday timeframe is served by two genuinely different mechanisms running side by side.

Intraday auctions (IDA) are periodic, centralized auctions — small siblings of the day-ahead auction. Everyone submits orders, the auction clears once, and it produces a single clearing price per product. There are three in the day's schedule:

AuctionGate closesCovers delivery
IDA1D-1 15:00 CETall of day D
IDA2D-1 22:00 CETall of day D
IDA3D 10:00 CETafternoon/evening of day D

Continuous intraday (CID) is the other animal entirely: an always-on order book where buyers and sellers post bids and offers and trades match the instant two of them cross — much like a financial exchange. It runs from after day-ahead opening right up to roughly one hour before delivery. There is no scheduled clearing moment. Trades simply happen, one after another, whenever someone lifts an offer or hits a bid.

That last sentence has a consequence most people miss the first time. Let's make you find it yourself.

Predict

You'll see trades for the same hour print at several different prices. Is one of them the "wrong" price?

Reveal

None of them is wrong. In a continuous book there is no single clearing price — each trade is its own private agreement between one buyer and one seller at the moment they crossed. A trade at 52 and a later trade at 55 are both completely valid; they just happened at different instants, against different resting orders, as the book firmed up.

This is the structural break from day-ahead. Day-ahead asks "what one price balances everybody at once?" The continuous book never asks that question. It just matches whoever is willing, whenever they're willing. So the phrase "the intraday price for hour 18" is, strictly, a fiction — and we'll have to deal with that fiction in Beat 04.

The contrast, made concrete

Here is a synthetic intraday auction for the same hour 18 — the IDA way. Participants submit buy and sell orders; the auction finds the one price where the most volume trades, and everyone transacts at that price. Run it and compare to the book you just watched.

Intraday auction (IDA) — delivery hour 18 (synthetic)

Sell offers stack from cheapest up; buy bids stack from highest down. The auction clears where they meet — one price for all.

The auction

One price, one moment

A single clearing price every participant pays or receives. Clean to quote, clean to settle — but you wait for the gate, and you only get one shot per auction.

The book

Many prices, all the time

Trade the instant you want — but every fill is its own price, and "the price" has to be reconstructed after the fact from all the trades.

BEAT 03 — CHOOSING A MECHANISM

It's 09:30 on delivery day. You're still adjusting hour 18.

New information has just landed and your position for hour 18 is off. IDA3 hasn't gated yet (it closes at 10:00). The continuous book is open.

Predict

Auction or book — and what does each one cost you to use?

There's no single right answer here. The point is to name the trade-off each mechanism forces.

Reveal

If you wait for IDA3, you get a clean single clearing price and the depth of everyone arriving at once — but you wait until the gate clears, and between now and then the information that worried you might move further. You're trading immediacy for a tidy price.

If you use the continuous book now, you act this minute — but you take whatever the book offers, at a price that's yours alone, and a big order may walk up through several levels (you saw that happen in Beat 02). You're trading a tidy price for immediacy.

That's the real decision intraday forces all day long: a clean price later, or my own price now. Neither is "correct" — it depends on how much your information is moving and how badly you need to be done.

Predict

These three numbers disagree. Which one is "the" intraday price?

Reveal

None of them, and all of them. There is no natural "the price" in a continuous book — there's only whichever rule you agreed to use, and the rule itself is a decision with consequences. Settlement might use one; a forecaster might use another; your risk system a third. They will not match, and that mismatch is real money.

This is also exactly why this lesson is built on synthetic data. The real Nordic intraday indices — the ones the market actually quotes — are commercial products from the exchanges, licensed and redistribution-restricted. We can teach you the structure for free; the live numbers themselves sit behind a contract. Knowing what those indices are made of, which is what you just did, is the part that's actually worth learning.

BEAT 05 — THE BATTERY WALKS THE BOOK

Who feels this most: the battery

Everything so far lands hardest on one kind of participant: the battery. A grid-scale storage unit (a BESS) has no fuel cost and responds in seconds, so its whole economics are temporal arbitrage — move energy out of cheap intervals and into dear ones. The fast, information-driven price moves you've been watching aren't noise to a battery; they're the entire opportunity.

Our charged battery reads exactly what you read in Beat 01 — wind dropped, hour 18 is firming — and wants to discharge into it. So it sells into the continuous book. But it's selling size, and you already know what that does here.

Predict — before you drag anything

The best bid resting in the book is €57/MWh. The battery sells 10 MW into it. Does it get €57?

Have a guess, then play with the widget below and come back.

Reveal

No — and the widget shows why. The battery takes the €57 bid first, but that bid is only 3 MW deep. To sell its full 10 MW it must walk down the book: 3 MW at €57, then 4 MW at €56, then 3 MW at €55. Its realised price is a VWAP across those fills — €56.00, a full euro below the best bid, and slipping further the more it sells. That gap is slippage: the direct cost of taking size in a continuous book.

This is the Beat 02 insight turned into money. The battery could instead wait for IDA3 and sell at a single clean clearing price — but waiting spends the closing window, and its state of charge plus the approaching gate make time itself expensive. A clean price later, or my own slipping price now: a battery lives that trade-off on every cycle.

And it rarely stops at intraday. A real BESS stacks revenue across day-ahead, intraday, and the balancing and ancillary markets — FCR-N, FCR-D, FFR, aFRR, mFRR — moving each MWh of energy and each MW of capacity to wherever it earns most at that moment. That multi-market puzzle is where the next lesson begins.

As ever: we're describing how a battery's economics meet these mechanisms — not handing you a strategy.

What to take with you

  • Intraday is the adjustment market — it exists because information keeps arriving after day-ahead has cleared.
  • It runs as two mechanisms. Auctions (IDA) give one clearing price at a gate; the continuous book (CID) gives immediate, per-trade prices all day.
  • The continuous book has no single price. "The intraday price" is always a chosen rule applied after the fact, never a fact in itself.
  • Choosing a mechanism is choosing a trade-off: a clean price later, or your own price now.
  • The battery lives here. A BESS arbitrages these fast moves, but taking size means walking the book — its realised price is a VWAP, and slippage is what immediacy costs.

Next door: the imbalance and balancing lesson, where we follow the power that's still out of position when the book closes and real time takes over — and where the battery's revenue stack (FCR-N, FCR-D, FFR, aFRR, mFRR) really comes into its own.

The rule, in one breathAn auction gives one price for the hour; the continuous book gives a different price for every trade — so “the” intraday price is only ever a rule you chose.
