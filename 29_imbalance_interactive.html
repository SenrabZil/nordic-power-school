Nordic Imbalance: When Real Time Takes Over

Every other market lets you choose whether to trade. This one doesn't. When the book finally closes and the grid has to balance second by second, the power you're still out of position on gets settled here — whether you meant to be in this market or not.

LEVEL · INTERMEDIATE PREREQUISITE · INTRADAY MENTOR · ELIZABETH CECILIA ~25 MIN
Education only

A teaching environment — nothing here is trading advice or a signal to act on. We read mechanisms and settlement, never what to do with them. Every figure is synthetic and deterministic, built to show structure, not any live market.

Where we are

After the book closes, the grid still has to balance

Continuous intraday shut about an hour before delivery. But electricity is consumed the instant it's produced — supply and demand have to match every single second, or the grid frequency drifts off 50 Hz. No market schedule can guarantee that. Forecasts are wrong, units trip, demand surprises.

So the TSO — Fingrid, Svenska kraftnät, Statnett, Energinet — steps in and physically balances the system in real time by activating reserves. That costs money, and the cost flows back to whoever was out of position, as the imbalance price. This is the backstop sitting behind day-ahead, intraday, everything.

BEAT 01 — THE MARKET YOU CAN'T LEAVE

Is your exposure over when intraday closes?

You've traded all the way through day-ahead and intraday. The continuous book just closed for delivery hour 18. You step away from the desk.

Predict — pick one

After intraday closes, is your exposure to this hour over?

The reasoning

Your exposure is never "over" — it just changes hands. Whatever gap remains between your final scheduled position and the power actually delivered is your imbalance, and eSett settles it automatically at the imbalance price for that 15-minute period. You don't opt in. If you're short, you effectively buy the shortfall here; if you're long, you sell the surplus here — at a price set by how expensive it was for the TSO to balance the whole system.

That's why this is the backstop behind every other market: every position you don't close in day-ahead or intraday lands here by default. Which makes the price it settles at worth understanding cold — so let's build it.

BEAT 02 — THE RESERVE CASCADE

Reserves don't arrive all at once

To hold 50 Hz, the TSO keeps layers of reserves on standby, each with a different speed and a different job. When something breaks, they come in in sequence: the fast ones first, to contain the fall, then slower ones to restore the frequency and free the fast reserves back up.

Predict — pick one

A 500 MW unit trips. What stops the frequency falling in the first seconds?

The reasoning

The first response is automatic. FFR and FCR sense the frequency dropping and inject power within a second or two — no instruction, no phone call. They contain the fall and hold a steady (if slightly low) frequency. Only then do the slower restoration reserves, aFRR and mFRR, pull frequency back to 50 Hz and release the fast reserves for the next event.

Watch it in the widget below, then note the last layer: mFRR is the manual workhorse — and its activation price is the one that flows straight into the imbalance price you'll settle against. That's Beat 03.

BEAT 03 — HOW THE PRICE FORMS

The marginal bid sets the price

That mFRR activation isn't free. Providers submit regulating bids, and the TSO activates them in merit order to cover the system's imbalance — cheapest first when the system is short and needs power up, most attractive first when it's long and needs power down. The last bid it has to activate — the marginal one — sets the balancing price, which becomes the 15-minute imbalance price.

Predict — pick one

The system is short by 300 MW. Which bids fire, and what sets the price?

The reasoning

Cheapest first, and the marginal activated bid sets a single price for everyone. Notice the echo: this is the auction logic from the intraday lesson — one marginal clearing price — not the fragmented, every-trade-its-own-price continuous book. When the system is short, up-regulation pushes the price above spot; when it's long, down-regulation drags it below spot, sometimes negative.

BEAT 04 — SINGLE PRICE, SINGLE POSITION

The same shortfall, two completely different bills

The Nordic model settles you on a single price and a single position: your production and consumption net to one imbalance figure, settled at one price regardless of which way you were off. So the question isn't just how far out you were — it's whether you were out the same way as the whole system, or the opposite.

Predict — pick one

You end the period 50 MWh short. Does it matter whether the system was short or long?

The reasoning

Take the slider above. With the system short, your 50 MWh shortfall settles at a high up-regulation price — you might pay €7,000. Now drag it to system long: the same 50 MWh short settles at a low or negative price, and you might receive €1,500. Identical position, opposite result.

Why? Because being short while the whole system was long means you actually helped balance it — you consumed less than planned exactly when there was a surplus. The single-price design rewards that alignment. The old two-price model used to split helpful and harmful imbalances; the single price folds it into one number, so the lesson is blunt: the imbalance price pays you for being on the system's side, not just for your volume.

BEAT 05 — WHOSE HOME TURF THIS IS

The battery, all the way down the stack

Remember the battery that walked the intraday book? This is where it really lives. A grid-scale BESS plays two roles at once: as a BSP (Balancing Service Provider) it sells reserve into the very cascade from Beat 02 — FCR-N, FCR-D, FFR, aFRR, mFRR — and as a BRP it arbitrages the imbalance price you just built.

Predict — pick one

Where does a grid battery earn the most?

The reasoning

The economics are a stack: capacity payments for holding reserve available (FCR-N/D, FFR, aFRR, mFRR), energy payments when that reserve is activated, and imbalance arbitrage on top — the battery shifts each MW and MWh to wherever it earns most at that moment. Reading the live system state — Fingrid publishes frequency and system imbalance on a 3-minute cadence — is how an operator anticipates whether the next period regulates up or down.

And one honest note to close on. Unlike the intraday continuous prices, which are commercial, this data is open: Fingrid publishes imbalance prices and real-time system state, and eSett publishes settlement. A learner could fetch the real series tomorrow. We used synthetic numbers only to keep the structure clean — and as ever, we've described how a battery's economics meet these mechanisms, not a strategy to run.

Carry this forward

What to take with you

  • Imbalance is the market you can't opt out of. Any gap between your schedule and physical delivery is settled, automatically, every period.
  • Reserves cascade by speed. FFR and FCR contain the fall in seconds; aFRR and mFRR restore 50 Hz over tens of seconds to minutes.
  • The marginal mFRR bid sets one imbalance price. Up-regulation lifts it above spot; down-regulation drags it below, sometimes negative.
  • Single price, single position. The same imbalance settles very differently by system direction — alignment with the system is what pays, not volume alone.
  • The battery lives across the whole stack — a BSP selling reserve, a BRP arbitraging imbalance.

You've now walked the full timeline: day-ahead clears the plan, intraday adjusts it, balancing holds it in real time, and settlement prices whatever's left. The capstone puts a single real episode through all four at once.
