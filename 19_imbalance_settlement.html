Lesson 19 · Imbalance, in detail

Settling
the gap.

Lesson 05 showed who pays and who earns. This one shows the machinery underneath: where the single imbalance price actually comes from, and who does the settling.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. The imbalance price isn’t a number handed down — it’s the cost of the last action the system took to stay at 50 Hz.

01

The balance responsible party

Every megawatt-hour on the system belongs to a balance responsible party (BRP), who commits a schedule before delivery. Afterward, meters record what actually happened. The difference, netted across the BRP’s whole portfolio for each settlement period — now 15 minutes, aligned to the market time unit — is its imbalance, settled at the imbalance price.

So the question is only: what is that price, and why?

02

Where the price comes from

When the system is short, the TSO activates up-regulation — climbing a merit order of balancing bids, cheapest first, just like the day-ahead stack but in real time. When long, it activates down-regulation, paying generators to back off. The single imbalance price is the price of the marginal activated bid: balanced, it sits at the spot; short, it climbs above; long, it falls below.

03

One price, both directions

The Nordic runs a single-price imbalance model: every BRP’s imbalance settles at that one price, whichever way it points. (Dual pricing, used in some markets, charges a worse price for the unhelpful direction.) Under single pricing your outcome turns on one thing — did your imbalance help the system or hurt it?

Take the price that just formed — spot €45, system short, imbalance price €65:

You long · system short

Your surplus is sold at €65 — you covered part of the deficit. You earn (helped).

You short · system short

You buy your shortfall at €65, well above spot — you deepened the deficit. You pay dearly (hurt).

You long · system long

If instead the system were long, your surplus sells at the low price — you added to the glut. You earn little (hurt).

You short · system long

You cover your shortfall at that low price — you absorbed the glut. You pay little (helped).

Same price, opposite outcomes — the single-price model quietly pays BRPs to imbalance in the direction the system needs.

04

Who actually settles it

Two different jobs, two different houses. Fingrid (and each Nordic TSO) publishes the imbalance price — the datasets you met in Lesson 05, consumption and production imbalance (92 / 93). eSett, the common Nordic settlement company owned by the TSOs, does the settlement: it nets every BRP’s metered position against its schedule across Finland, Sweden, Norway and Denmark, applies the price, and invoices.

That’s the split your data layer encodes: the price feed (Fingrid) and the settlement feed (eSett) are different sources for different facts — never silently merged.

05

Reading the imbalance price

Because the price is the cost of the last balancing action, the gap from spot is a live read on system stress: a price hugging spot means the system barely had to act; a price far above means it reached deep up the regulation stack to find megawatts. The sign tells you the direction — above spot, the system was short; below, long. Read the imbalance print and you’re reading how hard real time actually was.

This explains how the imbalance price forms and settles — not advice on running a balancing position or deliberately imbalancing. How a BRP manages its portfolio is a decision the school doesn’t make.

06

The rule, in one breath

The imbalance price is the marginal regulation cost; one price settles every BRP, and helping the system pays.

Short systems climb the up-regulation stack (price above spot), long systems descend the down stack (below). Under the Nordic single price, your imbalance earns or costs by whether it helped or hurt — and eSett nets and invoices it across the region while the TSOs publish the price.
