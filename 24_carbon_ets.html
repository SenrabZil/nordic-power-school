Lesson 24 · Carbon

The price
under the price.

Carbon never appears as a line on the curve. But it’s baked into the bid of every fossil plant, so it quietly sets how high the clear can climb. To read a thermal-set price, you have to read the carbon under it.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. What the EU ETS is, how it reaches the Nordic price, and when it bites.

Builds on · 03 Price formation

01

The hidden input

Every megawatt-hour from a fossil plant emits carbon, and in Europe that carbon has a price: an emission allowance, one per tonne of CO₂, that the plant must surrender. So a gas or coal unit’s cost isn’t just fuel and upkeep — it includes the cost of the allowances it burns through. That cost goes straight into the bid the plant offers into the auction.

You never see “carbon” on a price chart. But when a fossil plant is the marginal unit — the one that sets the clearing price (Lesson 3) — its allowance cost is inside that price. Carbon is the input under the input.

02

What the EU ETS is

The cost comes from the EU Emissions Trading System — a cap-and-trade market. Each year the EU sets a cap on total emissions, then issues that many allowances (EUAs). They reach the market mostly through a daily auction; some are still given free to industries at risk of relocating, though that free allocation is being phased out. Power and heavy industry, aviation and shipping — roughly ten thousand installations across thirty countries — must hold one allowance for every tonne they emit.

The cap shrinks each year, so allowances grow scarcer and — other things equal — the price climbs.

The cap falling over time is the whole design: scarcer allowances, a higher carbon price, a stronger push to burn less. In spring 2026 an allowance traded around €72 a tonne, inside a rough €60–95 band over 2025–26.

03

How carbon enters the power price

The mechanism is the merit order. A thermal plant bids its short-run cost: fuel, plus operations, plus its emissions multiplied by the allowance price. When that plant is marginal, its whole bid — carbon included — becomes the clearing price for everyone.

One marginal bid. The carbon slice sits on top of fuel and operations — raise the allowance price and the whole bid, and the clear, lifts with it.

Predict first: the allowance price doubles overnight. On a Nordic day where flexible hydro is the marginal unit in every hour, what happens to the price?

Little to nothing. Carbon only enters the price through the marginal plant. If hydro — which emits nothing — sets the price all day, the allowance price can do what it likes and the clear barely moves. Carbon bites only in the hours a fossil plant is on the margin.

04

Why the Nordics feel it less — but not nothing

That conditional is why a hydro- and nuclear-heavy system feels carbon more lightly than its neighbours. In Norway, Sweden and Finland a clean plant is marginal much of the time, so the allowance price often sits outside the clear. Coal- and gas-heavy zones — Poland, much of Germany — feel it in almost every hour.

But “less” isn’t “never.” Two routes bring carbon into the Nordic price anyway. Coupling: when the Nordics import from a continental zone whose thermal plant is marginal, that carbon-laden price sets the border (Lesson 21). And scarcity: on a cold, windless Dunkelflaute day (Lesson 22) gas runs at the margin even in the north, and its carbon cost lands squarely in the clear.

05

Level, not shape

Carbon behaves like fuel: it moves the level of the curve, not its shape. A higher allowance price lifts every hour where thermal is marginal, but it doesn’t change when the trough and peak fall — those are still drawn by demand and wind. This is exactly the fuel-shock case from the edge-cases lesson, with carbon as a co-author alongside gas.

Elizabeth: keep the two questions separate. Demand and weather ask what shape is the day. Fuel and carbon ask how high does that shape sit. Carbon is a height story, almost never a shape story.

06

What moves the carbon price itself

The allowance price isn’t fixed. The cap tightening each year pushes it up. A market stability reserve quietly removes allowances when there’s a glut and releases them when tight, damping the swings. Fuel switching matters: a high enough allowance price makes coal more expensive to run than gas, so plants switch and emissions fall. And the real economy matters — a recession, or a mild renewable-rich year, cuts emissions and with them the demand for allowances.

Predict first: a mild, windy year lets renewables cover far more of Europe’s demand, so power-sector emissions drop sharply. What tends to happen to the allowance price?

It tends to fall. Allowances are demand-driven: if the power sector emits less, it needs fewer of them, and the price eases — unless the stability reserve steps in to soak up the surplus. A clean year is bearish for carbon, all else equal. It’s the same logic in reverse as a cold, gassy winter, which is bullish.

07

What’s coming

Three changes are worth carrying. ETS2 is a second, separate carbon market for road transport and building heating fuels, launching in 2027 and placed upstream on fuel suppliers rather than households — its early price is expected far below the main market’s. CBAM, the carbon border adjustment, moved to full enforcement in early 2026, putting a carbon cost on imports of carbon-intensive goods so they don’t undercut EU producers. And the main system faces a 2026 review that could reshape the cap and the stability reserve.

None of these changes the mechanism you’ve learned — carbon still reaches the power price only through the marginal plant. They change how high, and how fast, that cost climbs.

08

Carbon is the price under the price

It never draws a line on the chart. It lives inside the bid of every fossil plant, and it reaches the clear only when one of them is marginal — often on the continent, sometimes in a cold northern scarcity, rarely on a clean hydro hour.

So as the system cleans up, carbon bites in fewer hours — but it sets the ceiling those hours can reach. Read whether the marginal plant is thermal, and you know whether carbon is in the room.

Demand draws the shape. Fuel and carbon set the height.
