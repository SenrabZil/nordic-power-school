Lesson 27 · Storage, sun & wind

Sun, wind, and
the battery between.

Solar and wind draw the shape of the day. A battery trades that shape — and by trading it, redraws it. These three aren’t separate assets bidding side by side; they’re one coupled system, each one’s value set by the others.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. How renewables make storage valuable, how storage erodes its own spread, and how it all gets captured.

Builds on · 13 BESS value stacking · 08 Spreads & duration

01

Three technologies, one curve

You’ve met all three already, separately. Solar digs a deep midday trough as every panel floods supply at once (Lesson 22, Lesson 25). Wind pushes residual load low at any hour and adds the day-to-day volatility (Lesson 20). A battery does the one thing neither can: it moves energy across time, buying when power is cheap and selling when it’s dear (Lesson 13).

The point of this lesson is that they meet on the same curve, and they need each other. The battery has nothing to trade without the troughs and peaks that solar and wind carve. And the troughs and peaks don’t stay put once a battery starts trading them. Read them together.

02

Renewables manufacture the opportunity

A battery’s energy revenue is a spread: the gap between the price it charges at and the price it discharges at. Anything that widens that gap makes the battery more valuable — and that’s exactly what solar and wind do. Solar craters the midday price; the evening ramp lifts the peak; wind throws in low-residual hours at odd times. Variable renewables don’t just coexist with storage — they manufacture the volatility storage lives on.

Predict first: a zone builds a lot more solar. What happens to a battery’s arbitrage revenue?

It rises. More solar means a deeper, more frequent midday trough and a sharper evening ramp — a wider spread for the battery to arbitrage. The more renewables a zone adds, the more valuable the first batteries become. Storage and solar are born partners: the resource that ruins solar’s own capture price (Lesson 25) is the resource that feeds the battery.

03

The daily dance

Put a battery on a high-solar day and its schedule writes itself from the curve. Through the cheap midday hours it charges — sometimes paid to take power, when the trough goes negative. As solar drops and demand climbs into the evening, it discharges into the peak. Its state of charge is a mirror image of the price: fill in the valley, empty on the hill.

━ price · ▮ charge · ▮ discharge · ┄ state of charge — the battery’s day is the price curve, read upside down.

Wind shifts the choreography. A windy night can be the cheapest window of all, so the battery charges overnight instead; a windless evening makes the peak sharper, so it saves its energy for then. The asset doesn’t have a fixed routine — it has a routine dictated by that day’s sun and wind.

04

The battery flattens what it trades

Here’s the twist that makes this a system and not a one-way street. When the battery charges in the trough, it adds demand there and lifts the price. When it discharges into the peak, it adds supply there and lowers it. The very act of capturing the spread narrows the spread — it raises the floor and caps the ceiling.

That has a generous side effect: by lifting the midday trough, the battery raises the price solar captures in its own hours. Storage is the single best antidote to solar’s cannibalisation (Lesson 25) — it buys exactly the energy solar is dumping, exactly when solar is dumping it. The two technologies improve each other’s economics while quietly eroding the gap the battery feeds on.

05

The spread is a shared, shrinking resource

If one battery narrows the spread a little, a fleet of them narrows it a lot. The arbitrage spread is a common pool: every battery that charges the trough and sells the peak takes a slice and leaves less for the next one. As storage builds out, spreads compress — toward the point where the marginal battery only just covers its round-trip losses and degradation.

Spread vs storage. Each gigawatt of storage shaves the spread; the curve flattens toward the marginal battery’s break-even.

Predict first: the same solar-heavy zone now also adds a large battery fleet. What happens to the arbitrage spread?

It shrinks. Every battery lifts the trough and caps the peak, so the spread they all share narrows — until the next battery barely breaks even after losses and wear. Storage erodes the opportunity it chases. This is the same merit-order logic as solar cannibalising itself: success competes the margin away.

06

Why they’re built together

Because the three are so coupled, they’re increasingly built as one project. Solar-plus-storage is the canonical pairing: the midday trough is predictable, so a co-located battery can soak the panels’ own output behind a shared grid connection, dodge curtailment, and shift it to the evening — turning a cannibalised midday megawatt into an evening-peak one. Wind-plus-storage is looser, because wind is less predictably shaped, but the battery firms a variable output and smooths its intraday swings. Either way the logic is the same: pair the thing that makes volatility with the thing that monetises it.

07

How the spread is actually captured

A battery doesn’t earn the single lowest trough price and the single highest peak print. It fills its charge and its discharge over a window, across many trades in the continuous intraday book. So what it really earns is the volume-weighted average price — the VWAP — of its charge window against the VWAP of its discharge window. Its margin is discharge-VWAP minus charge-VWAP, minus losses. The VWAP isn’t a benchmark bolted on afterwards; it’s the literal shape of the battery’s revenue.

Predict first: a battery fires its whole evening discharge in one large clip versus spreading it across the peak window. Which earns more?

Spreading wins. A single large sell into a thin book pushes the price down against itself — the battery eats its own slippage. Filling gradually across the window captures close to the peak’s VWAP without moving it. That’s why storage desks trade to VWAP: it’s both the thing they’re optimising (charge-VWAP vs discharge-VWAP) and the measure of whether they captured the spread cleanly.

08

Beyond energy: the balancing link

The interconnection runs through the balancing market too. Wind is the hardest technology to forecast, so when the wind lands far from plan, the system is short or long and imbalance prices move (Lessons 18–19). A battery’s speed makes it ideal for selling that flexibility — fast frequency response and reserves — so wind’s very unpredictability becomes a battery revenue stream, stacked on top of energy arbitrage (Lesson 13). Sun and wind make the volatility; the battery sells it twice, once as energy and once as balancing.

The loop: renewables widen the spread, storage captures and then flattens it, and the flatter, higher trough lets still more renewables onto the system.
09

Sun and wind draw the shape; the battery trades it; trading it redraws it

They are one system. Renewables manufacture the volatility — the troughs, the peaks, the negative hours — that gives storage something to trade. Storage captures that spread and, in capturing it, flattens it, lifting the price solar earns and shrinking the gap for the next battery.

So their fortunes are joined: more renewables make the first batteries rich; more batteries make renewables more bankable; and both together compress the spread toward the cost of the storage that closes it.

Never read the battery, the sun and the wind apart. The value of each is written by the other two.
