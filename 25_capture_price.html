Lesson 25 · Capture price

The price you get,
not the price you see.

A wind or solar farm doesn’t earn the average market price. It earns the price in its own generating hours — and those are exactly the hours its own output pushes down. That gap, and why it widens, is this lesson.

with Elizabeth Cecilia. Capture rate, the cannibalisation effect, and what reads through to a project’s economics.

Builds on · 03 Price formation · 07 Reading curves

01

Two different prices

There are two prices a generator can talk about. The average price is the simple mean of the market across all hours — what a perfectly flat, always-on plant would earn. The capture price is what a specific plant actually earns: the market price in each hour, weighted by how much that plant produced in that hour.

For a flat generator the two are identical. For wind and solar they pull apart — because wind and solar don’t produce evenly across the day. They produce in bursts, and the price in those bursts is what they capture.

02

The capture rate

Divide one by the other and you get the capture rate (or value factor): capture price as a percentage of the average price. Above 100% means a plant earns a premium — it produces when prices are high. Below 100% means a discount — it produces when prices are low. The capture rate is the single number that tells you how kindly the market treats a technology’s output pattern.

Solar’s problem in one picture. Output piles into the midday hours — the very hours its own supply has pushed the price down. The weighted capture sits below the flat average.
03

Why solar cannibalises itself

Solar has a brutal version of this. Every panel in a region generates at the same time — the middle of the day — so they flood supply together, push the merit order down together, and drive the midday price toward (and through) zero. Each plant then earns that depressed midday price on most of its output. The more solar a region builds, the deeper the midday hole and the lower the price every solar plant captures. Success eats itself.

Predict first: a region keeps adding solar, year after year. What happens to solar’s capture rate?

It falls — often sharply. German solar captured around 98% of the average price in 2022; by 2025 that had slid to roughly 54%, and in some markets it has gone far lower — Spanish solar captured about 9% in February 2026, down from over 70% a year earlier. More panels, more output crammed into the same hours, a deeper price hole: the capture rate falls as the build-out succeeds.

04

Why wind cannibalises less

Wind suffers the same effect, but more gently, for two reasons. It blows across the whole day rather than piling into one block, so its output isn’t all sold into a single low-price window. And wind isn’t correlated across countries the way the sun is — it can be still in Denmark and blowing in Scotland — so trade between zones spreads the output out, a smoothing called geographic averaging. The result is a higher, more stable capture rate than solar in most markets.

One market’s solar capture rate. The direction is the lesson: as installed capacity climbs, the rate slides.
05

The merit-order effect behind it

Underneath, this is just the merit order (Lesson 3) run forward in time. Each cheap renissance megawatt pushes the marginal unit one step down the stack, lowering the clear. At low penetration that mostly displaces expensive fossil plant — helpful, and the renewable still captures a decent price. At high penetration the renewable itself becomes the marginal unit and sets a low or negative price in its own hours. Cannibalisation is the merit-order effect turned back on the technology that caused it — the negative-price and solar-led days of Lesson 20 and Lesson 22 are the same story, seen from the producer’s side.

06

The Nordic angle

The Nordics have natural defences the solar-heavy continent lacks. Flexible hydro can hold water back during a glut and release it when prices recover, smoothing the trough. Deep interconnection and a spread-out wind fleet give geographic averaging. But the defences aren’t infinite: SE2 logged the most negative-price hours in Europe in 2025, and as Nordic solar and wind grow, capture rates there face the same gravity. For anyone living off merchant revenue or a market-linked contract, the capture rate — not the headline average — is the number that decides whether the asset pays.

This is a lesson in how the market values output patterns — not a view on which technology to build or back. The numbers describe what has happened; they are not a forecast, a recommendation, or investment advice.

07

What lifts a capture rate back up

Cannibalisation isn’t a one-way street. Anything that moves demand into the glut, or moves the glut’s energy into scarcer hours, lifts the captured price. Batteries are the clearest case: they buy the cheap midday and sell the expensive evening, which raises the trough and lowers the peak — the value-stacking of Lesson 13, seen from the system’s side. Demand flexibility, electrified heating and transport that can shift to sunny hours, more interconnection, and sector coupling all do the same.

Predict first: a large battery fleet is added to a solar-heavy zone. What happens to solar’s capture rate?

It rises. Batteries buy power in the midday glut — adding demand exactly when solar floods the market — which lifts the depressed midday price solar is selling into. The trough comes up, the capture rate improves. It’s why storage and solar economics are so tightly linked: each makes the other worth more.

08

Build the same thing everywhere and it’s worth less everywhere

A generator earns the price in its own hours, not the average — and variable renewables produce in the very hours their own output depresses. That is the cannibalisation effect, and it deepens as a technology succeeds.

Solar suffers it hardest because it all arrives at once; wind less, because it’s spread across time and geography; and anything that shifts energy — storage, flexible demand — pushes back against it.

Read the capture rate, not the headline price. It’s the number that knows whether the megawatt was worth making.
